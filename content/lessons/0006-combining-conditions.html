Two questions at once

In real life

A job advert says "You need a degree and two years' experience." Miss either one and you're out. A cinema says "Half price for children or students." Being either one is enough.

The Boolean operators and and or join two conditions into one:{{cite:1}}

  • A and B is True only when both are true.
  • A or B is True when at least one is true.

Every possible combination fits in a small table, called a truth table:{{cite:1}}

FalseTrueFalseTrue

cinema.py
age = int(input("Age? "))
student = input("Student (yes/no)? ")
if age < 12 or student == "yes":
    print("Half price")
else:
    print("Full price")
Shows (typed 25, then yes)Age? 25 Student (yes/no)? yes Half price

Python works out the comparisons first (age < 12 is False, student == "yes" is True), then combines them: False or True is True.{{cite:2}}

A common mistake

In English we say "if the day is Saturday or Sunday". In Python you must repeat the comparison on both sides: day == "Saturday" or day == "Sunday". Writing day == "Saturday" or "Sunday" doesn't do what you'd expect: it's always treated as true, because a non-empty string on its own counts as true.{{cite:6}}

not, and checking a range

In real life

"Not" flips an answer: if "the shop is open" is false, then "the shop is not open" is true. And a sign saying "for ages 13 to 19" is really two checks at once: at least 13, and at most 19.

not flips a Boolean: not True is False and not False is True.{{cite:1}} It reads naturally: if not raining:.

To check that a value sits between two limits, you can write it out in full with and, or use Python's shorter form, called a chained comparison:{{cite:4}}

teen.py
age = 15
print(age >= 13 and age <= 19)   # the long way
print(13 <= age <= 19)            # the same check, chained
ShowsTrue True

Anything after # on a line is a comment, just like a full comment line. Python ignores it.

The remainder operator: %

In real life

Share 17 sweets equally between 5 friends: each gets 3, and 2 are left over. That leftover, 2, is the remainder. If nothing is left over, the sweets shared out perfectly.

The % operator (say "modulo" or just "remainder") gives what's left over after dividing: 17 % 5 is 2.{{cite:5}} It's how programs answer questions about dividing evenly:

  • n % 2 == 0 asks "is n even?" (nothing left over when split in two).
  • n % 3 == 0 asks "is n divisible by 3?"
  • n % 2 == 1 asks "is n odd?"

New words

Boolean operator
and, or or not: combines or flips True/False values.
Truth table
A table showing the result for every combination of True and False.
Remainder
What's left over after dividing. % gives it: 17 % 5 is 2.
Divisible
Divides with nothing left over: n % d == 0.

Challenge: leap years

A year is a leap year if it's divisible by 4, except years divisible by 100 aren't, unless they're also divisible by 400. So 2024 is, 1900 isn't, and 2000 is. Ask Year? and print Leap year or Not a leap year.

Hint 1: break it into pieces

Write the three facts as conditions first: year % 4 == 0, year % 100 != 0, year % 400 == 0. A leap year is "divisible by 400" or "divisible by 4 and not by 100".

Hint 2: parentheses

Group the "and" part in parentheses, like in maths: A or (B and C). It makes the meaning obvious to anyone reading.
